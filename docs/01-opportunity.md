# 01 — The Opportunity for Small Retailers

## The market

- **AUD 11.94 billion** — value of the Australian gift card market in 2025
  ([Expert Market Research, 2025](https://www.expertmarketresearch.com.au/reports/australia-gift-card-market)).
- **60%+** of Australian gift card sales were already digital in 2023, showing a
  clear shift to online and contactless gifting (Australian Retailers
  Association, 2023).
- **~$1.4 billion** worth of gift cards go untouched across Australia
  ([Finder / CPB](https://www.cpb.com.au/forgotten-and-expired-gift-cards-leave-billions-unspent/)).
- **1 in 3 (33%)** Australians have at least one unused gift card; **21%** have
  two or more (Finder, 2025, n=1010).
- Almost **1 in 5** have had a card expire before they could use it (Finder).
- Men hold **$175** each in unredeemed cards on average; women **$186**. Women
  (24%) are more likely than men (19%) to let funds expire. Gen Z lose money far
  more often (27%) than Baby Boomers (14%) (Finder).

## Why small retailers specifically

The gift card problem is usually framed around big retailers, but small
retailers — the cafe, the coffee shop, the neighbourhood restaurant — are the
underserved edge:

- **They can't issue digital gift cards at all.** Their options today are paper
  gift certificates, a generic voucher from a stationery shop, or nothing.
- **Store credit is a scribble.** A refund a customer isn't entitled to under
  policy becomes a handwritten note or a coupon — easy to lose, easy to forget,
  and it reads as "no refund" to the customer.
- **No reminders, no data.** They have no way to nudge a customer to come back
  and spend, and no idea who is holding unspent value.
- **They won't build their own app.** A cafe is never going to invest in an app,
  and even if it did, customers won't download 100 single-store apps. This is
  exactly the gap a consolidated platform fills.

## The retention argument (why this isn't just breakage)

Unredeemed value ("breakage") is short-term revenue for a retailer, so on the
surface the incentive isn't perfectly aligned with Spendy's mission of getting
cards *spent*. The counter-argument is retention:

- Issuing **store credit** sees roughly **68%** of customers return for another
  purchase, versus **~45–50%** for cash refunds.
- Customers typically **spend above the credit amount** on their next visit — a
  $50 voucher often becomes a $100 basket. That upsell is worth more than the
  breakage.

> **Retention beats breakage long-term.** A spent card brings a customer back and
> grows the basket; an expired card is a one-time gain and a soured customer.

This model fits high-frequency, moderate-ticket retailers well (cafes,
casual dining) and fits poorly where it was never going to work anyway:

- **Endota Spa** — inherently expensive products, low repeat frequency.
- **Gucci** — high-end and personalised; a generic digital card cheapens it.
- **Mecca** — the positive case: internal KPIs push cards to be *spent*, and
  cards carry no expiry, so unredeemed value is a permanent balance-sheet
  liability. That's precisely the pain Spendy resolves.

## The flywheel

The small-retailer offering is not only a standalone B2B product — it is the
**cheapest customer-acquisition channel for the consumer app**:

1. A retailer issues a gift card or store credit through Spendy.
2. The recipient opens it in the Spendy app (the forced-download moment).
3. Spendy gains a consumer user who has money to spend and a reason to return.
4. That user redeems, spends above the card value, and the retailer sees the
   retention — so they issue more cards.

Each issued card acquires a consumer *and* proves the B2B value in the same
motion.
